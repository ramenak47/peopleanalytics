The exam tests whether you think like a People Analytics practitioner under pressure. What follows is the load-bearing knowledge, organised so concepts reinforce each other rather than sit as a list of summaries to memorise.

01

What People Analytics is, what it is not

People Analytics is the disciplined use of behavioural, operational, and HR data to test claims about how people drive organisational outcomes. The terminology is in flux. HR Analytics, Workforce Analytics, Talent Analytics, and People Analytics are often used interchangeably, and the distinction is more historical than substantive. “Workforce Analytics” emerged from software vendors in 1999. “Talent Analytics” appeared around 2006 with the rise of talent-management software. “People Analytics” was popularised around 2013 through Google’s data-driven HR work.

The cleanest definition synthesises what the field does rather than which label it carries. People Analytics is a practice enabled by information technology that uses descriptive, visual, and statistical analyses of data related to HR processes, human capital, organisational performance, and external benchmarks to establish business impact and enable data-driven decision-making.

HR Metrics vs HR Analytics

This is the distinction worth getting right. HR metrics are measures of key HR outcomes — turnover rate, training hours, time-to-hire. They describe. HR Analytics goes further by using statistical techniques, experimental approaches, and cross-functional data to establish business impact. Counting how many people left is a metric. Asking which working conditions predict departure, and what that costs the firm, is analytics.

The shape of the field

People Analytics sits where HR data meets operations data meets behavioural science. Pure HR data is rarely enough. The strongest analyses cross system boundaries, joining engagement surveys to production output, scheduling to inventory, recruiting to performance. A practitioner who only looks at HR data alone is doing HR reporting, not People Analytics.

Three families of question it answers

Workforce questions
Who do we have, who is leaving, who is engaged, who is being promoted? Demographics, turnover, engagement, mobility.
Performance questions
What predicts strong individual or team performance? How does staffing affect output? Where is productivity slipping and why?
System questions
Are HR policies (pay, training, scheduling, perks) producing the outcomes we intend, or unintended ones? This is where the most strategic work lives.
02

The four waves and the ACAI spiral

The textbook framing of analytics maturity comes in two flavours: a descriptive ladder (descriptive → diagnostic → predictive → prescriptive) and a strategic ladder (the four waves). Both matter, and they describe different things. The descriptive ladder is about what your analysis answers. The strategic ladder is about what your function is for.

The descriptive ladder

LevelQuestionTypical MethodExample
DescriptiveWhat happened?Counts, means, dashboardsTurnover rate last quarter
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Correlation, segmentation, regressionWhat variables co-move with turnover?
PredictiveWhat is likely next?Regression, classification, MLWho is at risk of leaving next quarter?
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Optimisation, simulation, experimentsWhat intervention reduces turnover cost-effectively?

The four waves of People Analytics

This is the strategic framing and it is exam-relevant. The field has historically moved through three waves and is now entering a fourth.

WAVE 01
Benchmarking

Comparing internal metrics to competitors or industry averages. Limit: being “average” does not differentiate.

WAVE 02
Best Practices

Mimicking industry leaders. Limit: another firm’s best practice may not fit your context or create unique value.

WAVE 03
Predictive

Modelling outcomes from past HR practices. Limit: emphasises interpreting the past more than creating the future.

WAVE 04
Guidance for Impact

Prescriptive, stakeholder-centric. Answers the “so that?” question, guiding investments that produce value for all stakeholders.

The Fourth Wave is the conceptual upgrade. Earlier waves answer “what happened” or “what is likely.” The Fourth Wave demands that every analysis end with “so that”. We report training investments so that customer retention improves by X%. We model leadership development so that investor confidence increases. Without the “so that,” analytics becomes activity rather than impact.

Push vs pull, and why the ladder is not linear

The standard textbook view treats analytics maturity as a staircase: organisations climb from basic reporting to sophisticated prediction. Modern frameworks reject this linearity. For an analytics function to mature, organisations must balance push factors (technical capabilities and skills of the analytics team) with pull factors (organisational demand for data-driven insights).

The push/pull diagnosis

Investing heavily in advanced predictive capabilities without corresponding demand from business leaders produces sophisticated reports nobody acts on. Conversely, intense demand from leadership without technical capacity produces fast, sloppy answers. The interesting failures in real organisations are almost always push/pull imbalances, not method failures.

The ACAI spiral

If the four waves describe where the field is going, ACAI describes how a team gets there. Not a staircase, a spiral. Each loop educates the business, raises the level of questions being asked, and matures the function.

A
Ask

The right questions. Stakeholder-relevant, decision-attached, not just data-available.

C
Collect

The right data. Linked to the question, minimum necessary, defensible in source.

A
Analyse

The right way. Method matched to question, transparent about uncertainty.

I
Influence

The right decisions. Findings translated, framed, and delivered to land action.

The cycle is iterative. Each completed loop changes what questions the business is capable of asking next. That is the maturity mechanism. Linear staircase models miss it because they assume capability builds in one direction; the spiral acknowledges that capability and organisational demand co-evolve.

03

Stakeholders of value

Old-school HR analytics looked inward — at employees, at HR professionals, at internal process. The Fourth Wave pushes the field to identify stakeholders of value: the diverse groups, internal and external, who receive sustainable benefit from human-capital investments. The strategic question is no longer “is our HR working?” but “is our HR producing value for whom, and is that the right whom?”

The framework identifies seven key stakeholder groups. Knowing the list is exam-useful; knowing what each group wants is what makes the framework operational.

External stakeholders

External · Capital
Investors

Long-term confidence and reduction of investment risk. Analytics provides data on intangible value — leadership quality, culture — that goes beyond financial statements.

External · Market
Customers

Shared value through a positive relationship. Analytics shows how frontline-staff capability and engagement drive customer satisfaction and revenue per customer.

External · Social
Communities

Reputation and corporate citizenship. Analytics measures human-capital impact on community perception and social-responsibility outcomes.

Internal stakeholders

Internal · Workforce
Employees

An enhanced employee experience: capability, well-being, growth. The primary participants in HR practice.

Internal · Governance
Board of Directors

Oversight of the human-capital agenda, leadership accountability, cultural health.

Internal · Executive
Senior Executives

Prescriptive analytics to prioritise human-capital investments (hiring, training) that deliver strategic outcomes.

Internal · HR
HR Professionals

Architects of the human-capital agenda. Analytics moves them from administrative support to strategic partners by linking practice to business success.

The pivot it forces

Analytics must start with stakeholder relevance rather than data abundance. The framework reframes outcome variables: what each stakeholder values is the dependent variable. Human-capital initiatives (talent, leadership, culture) are then tested as independent variables, predicting the value that matters to whom. This re-orients the entire analytic enterprise from internal HR efficiency to external impact.

A practitioner who can name these seven groups and articulate what each wants has the conceptual structure for almost any “why does HR matter strategically” exam question.

04

Conceptual frameworks — a working taxonomy

People Analytics has historically suffered from a thin theoretical base. Practitioners and researchers have responded by building a small library of frameworks that organise how the work gets done. Knowing the taxonomy is exam-useful in itself. Questions like “what frameworks would you draw on” want a structured answer, not a list of techniques.

The frameworks cluster into four families. Each family answers a different question. Process frameworks ask how do we run the analytical lifecycle. Strategic frameworks ask how does this connect to business value. Diagnostic frameworks ask where does performance come from. Persuasion frameworks ask how do findings actually move people.

Family 1 — Process and measurement

Structured models that guide the analytical lifecycle from question to decision.

LAMP Model — Logic, Analytics, Measures, Processes
A successful measurement system requires four components. Logic: the theory linking HR to outcomes. Analytics: the methods used to test that logic. Measures: the indicators chosen to operationalise constructs. Processes: the organisational routines that turn findings into decisions. LAMP’s contribution is naming Logic and Processes as equal partners to the technical work. Most failing analytics functions have decent Measures and Analytics but no Logic and no Processes.
HR Scorecard
Maps the causal linkages between HRM processes, human capital, and ultimate business outcomes. The scorecard logic ensures HR metrics align with strategic goals rather than measuring HR activity for its own sake. Think of it as a Balanced Scorecard adapted to HR’s contribution.
ACAI Model
See §02. The iterative spiral of Ask, Collect, Analyse, Influence. Treats analytics as a continuous scientific process rather than a one-off project. The feedback loop is the maturity mechanism.

Family 2 — Strategic and value creation

Frameworks rooted in strategic management. They ensure analytics is not an insular HR exercise but plugged into how the firm actually creates value.

People Analytics Stakeholder Framework
See §03. Seven stakeholder groups (investors, customers, communities, employees, board, executives, HR), each with distinct value demands. Pivots analytics from inward-looking efficiency to outward-looking value creation.
Resource-Based View (RBV)
A foundational strategic-management theory frequently applied to HR Analytics. RBV frames human capital as a unique, valuable, rare, and hard-to-imitate internal resource. Sustained competitive advantage comes from optimising these resources. The implication for analytics: human capital is not a cost to minimise but a resource to allocate strategically. Barney’s 1991 paper is the canonical reference.

Family 3 — Diagnostic and change management

Once data is analysed, frameworks from organisational behaviour diagnose where the problem actually lives and drive change.

Ability-Motivation-Opportunity (AMO) Theory
Workforce performance decomposes into three questions. Ability: can they do it? (skills, training, selection). Motivation: do they want to do it? (incentives, engagement, recognition). Opportunity: will the system let them? (workflow, autonomy, culture, removed obstacles). AMO prevents the most common diagnostic error in HR: assuming the problem is motivation when it is actually opportunity, or assuming it is ability when it is actually motivation. Any performance question worth answering should be passed through AMO before intervention is proposed.
Organisational Sensemaking
Analytical findings do not automatically trigger managerial change. Karl Weick’s sensemaking literature shows that organisations have to collectively make sense of new information before they act on it. The operational sequence: Envisioning (using data to focus leadership attention on a trigger), Signalling (communicating the data-driven need for change), Re-visioning (fostering collective understanding of what the change means), Energising (controlling the new narrative and sustaining momentum). Findings without a sensemaking process attached typically die in committee.

Family 4 — Persuasion and communication

Frameworks from cognitive psychology and communications. They exist because people often reject data that threatens their beliefs, self-interest, or identities — a phenomenon called motivated reasoning. The persuasion frameworks treat this as a design problem, not a deficit in the audience.

Jiu-jitsu persuasion
See §09. Rather than arguing directly against resistance, identify the underlying motivation or fear and align the data’s message with it. Shifts the conversation from zero-sum conflict to a “both-and” solution.
Storytelling structures
See §09. Three-Act Structure (setup, conflict, resolution); Bing-Bang-Bongo (preview, deliver, summarise); horizontal and vertical logic. Cuts through cognitive resistance that bulleted facts cannot.
How to deploy this taxonomy

A strong exam answer to “what frameworks would you use” names the family first, then picks one or two frameworks from each family relevant to the question. Process frameworks tell you how to run the analysis. Strategic frameworks tell you what it should be for. Diagnostic frameworks tell you where the problem actually lives. Persuasion frameworks tell you how to make the findings stick. A practitioner who only uses one family produces lopsided work.

05

Diagnosing a business problem

Before any model, there is a translation step. A business problem (“we are losing bottles”, “we are losing salespeople”, “our engagement scores dropped”) has to become an analytical problem with a defined outcome, unit of analysis, and method. This is where most weak analyses go wrong: they jump to a method without specifying what they are measuring or why.

From business question to analytical design

→
Stakeholder value at stake
→
Outcome variable
→
Unit of analysis
→
Candidate explanations
→
Method
→
Decision

Each arrow is a defensible choice you should be able to justify. The board does not need to see your regression. They need to see that you knew what you were measuring and why.

Specifying the outcome variable

The most common error is treating a raw observable as the construct of interest. A “missing bottles” figure bundles breakage, scanning error, registered perks, and possible informal taking. A “turnover rate” bundles regretted and unregretted departures, voluntary and involuntary. A careful analyst constructs a residual after netting out what is already explained, and works on that.

Construct vs. measure

The construct you care about (informal leakage, regretted attrition, true engagement) is not the same as the measure you have (a number on a spreadsheet, a survey response). A serious analyst is upfront about this distance. Whatever moral story stakeholders read into the measure is a construct they import.

Grouping explanatory variables

Group them. It helps the reader and disciplines you. A reusable four-bucket grouping:

Operational factors

Process variables, equipment, throughput, downtime, error rates. The “the system itself failed” family.

Workforce factors

Tenure, training, overtime, temp share, absence, shift. The “the people composition matters” family.

Policy / system factors

Compliance, registered perks, formal procedures. The “the rules-of-the-game” family.

Context factors

Time, season, product type, customer pressure, market conditions. Controls that let you compare like with like.

The relationships worth investigating

  • Main effects: does each factor predict the outcome?
  • Interactions: does the effect of one factor depend on the level of another?
  • Nested patterns: are some units structurally worse, or worse because of the workload they handle?
  • Residual analysis: after legitimate sources are accounted for, is there still an unexplained signal?

What additional data would help

An exam-strong answer always considers what would improve the analysis. Useful additions typically include: timestamped automated data (heavy ethical cost), instrument calibration logs, supplier or process tolerances, employee survey data on perceived fairness, comparable benchmarks from other firms, baseline data from before the problem emerged. The point is to show that more data is not always better: each addition carries privacy, cost, and interpretive baggage.

Variables, units, and measurement

You should be fluent in classifying variables because the classification dictates which methods are legal.

TypeDescriptionTypical examples
Categorical (nominal)Labels without orderDepartment, location, role
Categorical (ordinal)Labels with orderJob level, Likert-scale agreement
ContinuousReal-valued, any number in a rangeSalary, tenure, percentages
CountNon-negative integersNumber of absences, requisitions filled
BinaryTwo-state outcomeLeft or stayed, promoted or not

Unit of analysis

What does one row of your data represent? An employee? A shift? A team-month? The unit of analysis disciplines how strong your conclusions can be. Claims at the aggregate level cannot be assumed to hold at the individual level, and vice versa.

The ecological fallacy

Drawing conclusions about individuals from aggregate-level data is called the ecological fallacy. If teams with more temp workers have lower output, this does not mean individual temp workers are responsible. The aggregate pattern is consistent with many individual stories. One of the most common reasoning errors in workplace analytics.

The say–do gap

HR analytics over-relies on self-reported survey data — engagement surveys, pulse surveys, exit interviews. These are highly susceptible to the say–do gap: what employees report does not always match what they do. An employee who reports high engagement may already be interviewing elsewhere. A serious analyst triangulates: survey data alongside behavioural data (attendance, output, internal moves) alongside outcome data. No single source is enough.

Sample size and statistical power

What looks like “lots of data” thins quickly when split by relevant subgroups. 600 shift observations divided across 3 lines × 3 shifts × multiple products × multiple months produces small cells. Effect estimates in small cells are noisy and confidence intervals widen. Be alert to claims that emerge only in narrow intersections.

06

Statistical methods to recognise

You will not derive anything. You need to name the right tool for the job and explain what it tells you.

Descriptive statistics

Means, medians, standard deviations, percentages, distributions. Always the first step. If a pattern only emerges in a regression and not in the descriptives, be suspicious. Descriptive tables and plots tell you whether the question is even worth modelling.

Correlation

Pearson’s r measures whether two continuous variables move together, bounded between −1 and 1. The cheapest diagnostic available. Correlation is not causation: two variables can move together because one causes the other, because both are caused by a third (confounding), or by chance.

Group comparisons

Comparing means across groups uses the t-test (two groups) or ANOVA (three or more). The result is a p-value: a statement about how surprising the data would be under a null hypothesis of no effect, not a statement about practical importance.

Linear regression

The workhorse. You model an outcome y as a weighted sum of predictors plus error. Each coefficient is the expected change in y for a one-unit change in that predictor, holding the others constant. That “holding constant” phrase does the moral work of the whole exercise.

Read coefficients carefully

A regression coefficient is a statement about this dataset and this model specification. Add or drop a variable and coefficients can flip sign. This is not a bug, it is the geometry of conditional relationships. Robustness checks — running multiple model specifications — are how analysts stress-test conclusions.

Logistic regression

When the outcome is binary (left vs stayed, promoted vs not), linear regression is not appropriate. Logistic regression models the log-odds of the outcome. Coefficients translate to odds ratios. The underlying logic — predictors explaining variation in the outcome conditional on other predictors — is the same.

Multilevel / hierarchical models

HR data is almost always nested: employees within teams, teams within units, observations within time periods. Standard regression assumes independence between observations. Multilevel models account for nested structure by allowing intercepts (and sometimes slopes) to vary by group. Mentioning multilevel models on an exam is a signal of statistical sophistication.

Experimental and quasi-experimental designs

The gold standard for causal inference is the randomised experiment (A/B test, randomised controlled trial). Where randomisation is impossible, quasi-experimental methods (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables) approximate it. People Analytics is mostly observational, which limits how strongly causal language can be used.

What you would not do

You would not jump to neural networks or random forests for a small, interpretable HR question. People Analytics rewards interpretability: the audience needs to understand the result, not just believe it. Black-box models also expose you to algorithmic-bias risks that interpretable models surface earlier.

07

Confounding, mediation, moderation

These three words separate careful analysts from sloppy ones. Each is a different relationship between three or more variables. Confusing them produces wrong recommendations.

Confounding
A third variable causes both the predictor and the outcome, creating a false impression that the predictor causes the outcome. Example: senior employees both earn more and have higher performance ratings. Tenure is a confounder for the salary–performance link.
Mediation
The predictor causes a second variable, which causes the outcome. The mediator is the mechanism. Example: high overtime → fatigue → lower output. Overtime affects output through fatigue. Knowing the mediator tells you where to intervene.
Moderation (interaction)
The effect of one predictor on the outcome depends on the level of another. Example: training only improves performance when managers actively support it. The relationship is conditional. Moderation explains why a single intervention can produce wildly different results across teams.
“Correlation is not causation” is a slogan. The discipline is naming, before you run anything, which of these three relationships you think is operating, and what would distinguish them in your data.

Visualisation for decisions

A chart for executives does three things: it makes the central pattern visible without explanation, it is honest about uncertainty, and it points the eye at the lever the audience can pull. Visualisation is not decoration. It is an argument for an action.

Match the chart to the message

GoalChart typeWhy
Show rankingHorizontal bar chartEasy comparison of magnitudes across categories
Show distributionBoxplot, histogramReveals spread, outliers, skew
Show change over timeLine chartTrends and inflection points
Show two-variable relationshipScatter with fit lineLinear or nonlinear association
Show interactionSmall multiples, or heatmapHow the effect of A depends on the level of B
Show decompositionStacked or waterfall chartHow a total breaks into components

Preattentive attributes

Human short-term memory processes about four chunks of visual information at a time. Good visuals reduce cognitive load by leveraging preattentive attributes — features the brain processes before conscious attention engages. The most powerful are size, colour, spatial position, and line width. Designed well, these direct the audience’s attention before they realise they are looking.

On colour, specifically

Colour should never be used to make a chart “colourful.” A rainbow palette prevents any single data point from standing out — everything competes for attention. Better: design in muted tones (grey) and use a single bold colour to draw the eye exactly to the data point that supports your narrative. Avoid red/green combinations, which fail for colour-blind viewers; rely on saturation contrast instead.

The Z-pattern and prime real estate

Audiences scan pages in a zigzag “Z” pattern starting from the top left. The most important information should be positioned in that prime real estate. The bottom right is the last place the eye lands — useful for the call to action, weak for the key insight.

Channels of encoding

Tamara Munzner’s rule of thumb: position is the most accurate visual encoding (used in scatter plots and bar charts). Length is next. Colour intensity is among the weakest channels for quantitative data — the brain is bad at translating shade differences into magnitudes. Use position for the variable you care about most. Reserve colour for highlighting, not for quantifying.

What the chart should support, decisionally

This is the part students miss. A chart is an argument for an action. Before designing it, name the action it should support: a specific intervention, an investment, a policy change. If the chart cannot be linked to a decision, it is decoration and should be cut.

09

Storytelling and persuasion

Elegant statistical insights are functionally useless if they cannot be understood and embraced by decision-makers. Moving analytics from insight to impact requires teams to leave behind conventional academic reporting and embrace storytelling, visual design, and psychological persuasion.

Storytelling over conventional rhetoric

Analysts often rely on bulleted facts, statistics, and complex tables. Business leaders are rarely moved to action by reason alone. Narratives reduce cognitive resistance, prevent counter-arguing, and increase long-term memory retention. The brain is built for stories more than for tables.

The Three-Act Structure

Act 1 — The setup
Establish context and introduce the imbalance or conflict. What is the business problem, and why now?
Act 2 — The development
Develop the tension. What are the consequences of inaction? What data-backed options exist? This is where analysis lives.
Act 3 — The resolution
Resolve the tension with a specific call to action. Not “here are some findings.” A concrete, costed, owned proposal.

Bing, Bang, Bongo

The principle of repetition. Tell them what you are going to tell them (executive summary). Tell them (detailed content). Tell them what you told them (closing summary). Audiences in long meetings retain only a fraction of what is said. The repetition is not condescension. It is how memory consolidation works under conditions of competing attention.

The One-Slide approach

Decision-makers rarely care about the granularity of statistical methods. Reduce findings to one slide: the proposed intervention, the cost, the expected impact on stakeholder value. Use intuitive analogies. Trade technical accuracy for communicative force, with full documentation available for those who want to dive deeper.

Structuring presentations with logic

Horizontal logic
Slide titles should be action titles, not descriptive labels. A reader should be able to skim only the titles from start to end and follow the entire story. “Q4 results” is a label. “Turnover dropped 18% after the manager-training pilot” is a horizontal-logic title.
Vertical logic
Within a single slide, everything must reinforce the title. Text supports the title. Visualisations support the text. Nothing extraneous. If something does not support the title, it leaves the slide.

Anticipating resistance

People resist data that threatens their beliefs, identities, or interests. Bombarding a resistant audience with more facts makes them dig in. Two techniques work better.

Two-sided arguments
For contentious findings, actively present the opposing arguments and then refute them with the best available evidence. A one-sided presentation reads as advocacy; a two-sided one reads as honesty and is harder to dismiss.
Jiu-jitsu persuasion
Rather than attacking a stakeholder’s surface attitude, identify the underlying motivation or fear driving their resistance. Align the data’s message with that underlying motivation. The conversation shifts from zero-sum conflict to a collaborative “both-and” solution. Named after the martial-arts principle of using an opponent’s momentum rather than opposing it directly.
The point about persuasion

Communication is not the last step of an analysis. It shapes whether analysis becomes action. A statistically perfect finding that no one acts on has produced zero value. A modest finding that produces a real decision has produced real value. The communication step is not auxiliary. It is the value-creation step.

10

Implementation challenges

The literature is consistent on what stops People Analytics from delivering value. The challenges cluster into six families, and a strong exam answer will name several of them.

1. The skills gap and function hijacking

The most cited barrier. Less than a third of HR analytics professionals report competency in advanced multivariate statistics. When HR cannot do the analytics, responsibility migrates to IT or Finance. Those functions often misunderstand human capital — treating labour as a fixed cost to be controlled rather than a flexible, value-producing resource. The analytical lens determines the answers; if Finance owns the lens, the answers are financial.

2. Misplaced focus and low-value analytics

Many teams stay stuck in basic reporting. A common figure: People Analytics teams spend 80% or more of their time on routine reports (headcount, turnover, training hours) rather than the strategic “so what?” questions that deliver value. Teams analyse data because they have it — building “warehouses of data” without business impact — or pursue academically rigorous work that is managerially irrelevant because analysts are isolated from business leaders. The corrective is the courage to say no to low-value requests and free the team from operational reporting.

3. Data quality and technological limitations

HCM systems and IT infrastructure that are meant to enable analytics often impede it. Data is siloed, inaccurate, or poorly integrated across regions and business functions. Over-reliance on subjective self-reported survey data produces the say–do gap. Behavioural and outcome data — what people actually do, what actually results — are harder to capture but more truthful.

4. The push/pull imbalance

Already discussed in §02. Investing in advanced predictive capabilities when the organisation does not demand them produces sophisticated unread reports. Conversely, intense demand without capacity produces fast wrong answers. The interesting failures are imbalances, not method failures.

5. Managerial resistance and algorithmic aversion

Two distinct phenomena often confused.

Stubborn reliance on intuition
Managers reject validated research (for instance, that structured interviews and cognitive tests predict job performance far better than unstructured interviews) because the evidence threatens autonomy, self-image, or interests. This is the Rynes et al. terrain (§12).
Algorithmic aversion
Specifically the distrust of algorithmic recommendations. Employees and managers often view human judgement as fairer or more genuine than algorithmic output, even when the algorithm is empirically more accurate. Aversion also reflects rational fear that algorithms will be used for surveillance, deskilling, or job elimination. Worth distinguishing from general resistance to evidence: aversion is targeted at the form (machine) as much as the substance.

6. Ethical risks and baked-in bias

The most consequential category. Models trained on historical data inherit the biases of that history. A predictive model of “leadership potential” trained on the firm’s past leadership will conclude that the demographic profile of past leaders (typically: men, typically: a narrow ethnic and educational profile) is the leadership profile. The model launders historical discrimination into the appearance of objectivity. Without theoretical grounding and ethical oversight, the model scales bias rather than reducing it. Section 11 develops this further.

The implementation diagnosis

When a People Analytics function is failing, the failure is almost never the methods. It is one of: insufficient skills, the wrong questions, broken data plumbing, push/pull imbalance, audience resistance, or unaddressed ethical risk. Name the actual failure mode before prescribing a fix.

11

Ethics, proportionality, surveillance

This is the section to take seriously. The political-economic stakes of People Analytics are not adjacent to the technical work — they shape what the technical work means.

The core ethical frame

Legitimate purpose
Is the question one the organisation has a defensible reason to ask? “Are we losing inventory?” is legitimate. “Which individual employees are insufficiently loyal?” is not.
Proportionality
Is the intrusion into employee life proportional to the magnitude of the problem? A modest unexplained gap does not justify constant surveillance. The means must match the size of the end.
Fairness and non-discrimination
Will the analysis produce conclusions that disproportionately harm a vulnerable or protected group? Temp workers, junior employees, and minorities are typically more precarious. A finding that correlates these groups with negative outcomes can quickly become a hiring rule that punishes people for being in those categories.

GDPR and the European context

In the Netherlands and across the EU, employee data is governed by the GDPR. Principles worth naming:

  • Lawful basis: processing must rest on a defined legal basis. Employee consent is contested as a basis because the power imbalance makes consent unreliable.
  • Purpose limitation: data collected for one purpose (scheduling) cannot be repurposed (investigation of individuals) without justification.
  • Data minimisation: only data necessary for the stated purpose should be collected.
  • Transparency: employees should know what is being analysed and why.
  • Works council consultation: in the Dutch context, the Ondernemingsraad must typically be consulted on significant uses of employee data.

Baked-in bias and the historical-data problem

A model that predicts leadership potential from past leaders learns the demographic shape of the past, not the structure of leadership. Without intervention, it scales discrimination. The Amazon recruiting tool that learned to downweight CVs containing the word “women’s” is the canonical case. The lesson is not that algorithms are uniquely biased — humans were already biased, which is why the training data looks the way it does. The lesson is that algorithms scale existing bias to a level of efficiency previously impossible.

The framing problem

How you label a finding matters as much as what you find. “Missing units are concentrated on Line C” is a description. “Line C workers are stealing” is an accusation, and one the data typically does not support. A responsible analyst refuses the loaded label and chooses the structural framing.

From a critical perspective

Workplace analytics descends in part from Taylorist scientific management and from older traditions of surveillance over labour. The HR analytics literature tends to present itself as neutral and technocratic, but the framing “are employees disloyal?” is structurally different from “is our process working?” The first individualises a structural issue. The second locates accountability at the system level. Choosing the second is itself an ethical decision.

What responsible practice looks like

  • Investigate at the system level first (process, training, compliance, policy clarity) before individualising.
  • Aggregate and anonymise. Never report at individual level unless there is specific cause and due process.
  • Consult employee representatives before extending data collection.
  • Audit predictive models for disparate impact before deployment, and again periodically after.
  • Be transparent with staff about what was found and what is changing. This is a labour-relations question as much as an analytics question.
  • Refuse low-value, high-intrusion analyses. The most ethical decision is sometimes to not run the analysis at all.
12

Resistance, Rynes et al., persuasion

Rynes, Colbert & O’Boyle (2018) sit at the centre of a recurring exam concern: the “research–practice gap.” The core observation is that managers routinely ignore or reject evidence-based findings even when the evidence is robust. The article diagnoses why, and proposes strategies for closing the gap.

Why managers resist evidence

Cognitive reasons
Confirmation bias (seeking information that confirms what we already believe); availability bias (vivid anecdotes outweigh statistics); the seduction of intuition built from personal experience that does not generalise.
Motivational reasons
Findings can threaten identity (“I have been doing this wrong for twenty years?”), challenge status, or imply costly change. Resistance is often emotional before it is intellectual.
Structural reasons
Managers face time pressure, lack training in research methods, may distrust academics, and operate under career incentives that reward decisiveness over reflection.
Communication reasons
Researchers often present findings in jargon-heavy, hedged language that does not travel to managerial audiences. Evidence loses on form before it is judged on substance.

Strategies Rynes and colleagues recommend

  1. Anticipate which findings will be resisted. Findings that contradict popular practice, threaten autonomy, or imply costly change face more resistance.
  2. Translate into the language of practice. Avoid jargon. Use vivid examples and concrete numbers managers can act on.
  3. Address self-interest and identity. Frame findings so they do not require managers to admit personal failure. Locate causes in systems, not individuals.
  4. Use credible messengers. Peer managers and respected internal voices persuade better than analytics teams. Build coalitions.
  5. Build evidence-based mindsets through repeated exposure. One presentation will not convert a sceptic. Embedded routine shifts norms over time.
  6. Acknowledge uncertainty honestly, without abandoning the conclusion. Candour about limitations builds credibility.
  7. Tie evidence to actual decisions. Generic findings drift. Findings attached to a live decision land.
The deeper point

Rynes et al. are not saying managers are irrational. They are saying that the conditions under which managers receive and use evidence are not the conditions under which evidence is produced. Closing the gap is a translation problem, an institutional problem, and a relational problem — not just a data problem.

Connecting Rynes to storytelling and jiu-jitsu persuasion

Section 09 introduced storytelling and jiu-jitsu persuasion as techniques. They are not separate from Rynes — they are the operational toolkit for what Rynes recommends. Anticipating resistance means designing communication that uses two-sided arguments, peer messengers, action-titled slides, decomposition charts that locate causes structurally, and language that aligns with the audience’s underlying motivations. The intellectual diagnosis (Rynes) and the practical techniques (storytelling, jiu-jitsu) are the same project.

Futures of the field

Where People Analytics is going. Useful for exam questions framed in terms of “what next” or “how should organisations prepare.” Five themes recur in the literature.

1. The move to Guidance for Impact

The Fourth Wave is the field’s strategic direction. Every analysis should end with “so that.” Report training investment so that customer retention rises X%. Model leadership development so that investor confidence increases. Without the “so that,” analytics remains activity rather than impact. Value will increasingly be measured by impact on external stakeholders (investors, customers, communities) and senior internal leadership (boards, executives), not just HR professionals.

2. AI and the duality of digital

AI and generative technologies are the primary driver of change. Two dynamics:

Doing digital — automation
AI automates repetitive administrative tasks (CV screening at scale, scheduling, basic reporting). This is efficiency work.
Being digital — augmentation
AI augments human capability by freeing professionals from low-value work, letting them focus on higher-value strategic work. This is leverage work.

The exam-relevant tension is algorithmic aversion: stakeholders often distrust AI-driven insights even when those insights are more accurate, viewing human judgement as fairer or more genuine. Managing this aversion is itself a People Analytics problem.

3. The impermanence of work

Traditional HR analytics measured permanent employees. Work is increasingly project-based, freelance, gig-based, contractor-heavy. Analytics must shift accordingly.

  • Project-based focus: tracking competencies, performance, and availability across a flexible talent pool rather than headcount in fixed roles.
  • Nimble modelling: real-time supply/demand matching, more sophisticated econometric models, faster cycle times.
  • New ethical questions: gig workers typically have weaker legal protections than employees, raising the stakes of how their data is used.

4. Bridging the academic–practice gap

The Rynes et al. agenda taken seriously. Two practical directions:

  • Co-creation of research: practitioners contribute business problems, academics contribute method. Findings emerge that are both rigorous and actionable.
  • Persuasive communication: against the broader cultural backdrop of declining trust in expertise (the “death of expertise”), analytics professionals must master storytelling and jiu-jitsu persuasion. Rigour without rhetoric loses.

5. Continuous maturation via the ACAI spiral

The maturity model of the future is not a staircase. It is a spiral. Each loop of Ask, Collect, Analyse, Influence educates the organisation, raises the level of questions being asked, and matures the function. The analytics team’s job is not only to answer questions but to cultivate the demand for better questions. This is what the literature means by “educating the market.”

A synthesising line

The future of People Analytics is prescriptive, stakeholder-centric, AI-augmented, ethically constrained, and rhetorically sophisticated. A field that began counting headcount is becoming a field that guides human-capital investment toward differentiated, measurable value for multiple stakeholders. Most organisations are not yet doing this. The exam reward is in being able to describe why, and what would change it.

Wider intellectual surroundings (beyond the exam)

The exam tests a specific slice. A comfortable practitioner has the surrounding terrain in their head. Below is the landscape — knowing this means the exam feels like a familiar neighbourhood rather than a stress test.

Where People Analytics came from

Three intellectual streams converge. Industrial-organisational psychology contributed the measurement of work behaviour (job analysis, performance ratings, engagement). Operations research and management science contributed the optimisation lens (staffing, scheduling, supply chains). Business analytics and data science contributed the computational tooling. The synthesis is recent (largely post-2010) and uneasy. Each parent discipline has different norms for what counts as evidence and how to relate to workers.

Scientific management as ancestor

Frederick Taylor’s Principles of Scientific Management (1911) is the deep ancestor of workforce analytics. Taylor decomposed work into measurable steps, timed them, and reorganised for output. The Hawthorne studies (1924–1932) at Western Electric pushed back, suggesting workers respond to being observed and that social dynamics matter. The tension between Taylor and Hawthorne — workers as measurable inputs vs. workers as social agents — runs straight through to current People Analytics debates.

Evidence-based management

The broader intellectual movement Rynes et al. belong to. Key figures: Denise Rousseau (her 2006 Academy of Management presidential address is foundational), Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert Sutton (Hard Facts, Dangerous Half-Truths, and Total Nonsense). The core claim: management decisions should be informed by the best available scientific evidence, organisational facts, practitioner judgement, and stakeholder values — not by fashion or anecdote.

Causal inference, briefly

Most People Analytics work is observational, not experimental. Causal claims are precarious. The serious literature draws on Judea Pearl’s work on directed acyclic graphs, Donald Rubin’s potential outcomes framework, and quasi-experimental designs. You do not need to do these on the exam. You need to know they exist and that observational analyses without them produce weaker conclusions than experiments.

Algorithmic fairness in HR

A growing literature on what happens when algorithms make HR decisions. The Amazon recruiting tool that penalised “women’s” on resumes is the canonical case. Frameworks: disparate impact vs disparate treatment, demographic parity vs equalised odds, the impossibility theorems showing you cannot satisfy all reasonable fairness criteria simultaneously. Cathy O’Neil’s Weapons of Math Destruction popularised the concerns.

The labour-process tradition

A counter-tradition reading workplace analytics as part of a longer history of managerial control over labour. Harry Braverman’s Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974), Michael Burawoy’s Manufacturing Consent (1979), more recent work by Karen Levy on truckers and Alex Rosenblat on Uber drivers. These authors do not deny that analytics works. They ask: works for whom, at whose expense, with what effect on the relationship between employer and employed?

The metrics paradox

Jerry Muller’s The Tyranny of Metrics documents how measuring something often distorts the thing being measured (Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure). Introduce a turnover target for managers and turnover will reliably drop — not because retention improves, but because reporting behaviour adapts. This is not cynicism. It is how complex systems respond to measurement.

People Analytics maturity in practice

Most organisations are stuck at descriptive analytics. Few make it to diagnostic. Genuine predictive and prescriptive work is rare, concentrated in large technology firms (Google’s People Analytics team, the canonical “Project Oxygen” study on what makes managers effective). A typical mid-sized firm doing diagnostic work competently is already doing better than industry average.

Key texts to know by name

  • Boudreau & Ramstad, Beyond HR: The New Science of Human Capital — the talentship framework, the idea that HR should think strategically about where talent makes the biggest difference.
  • Davenport, Harris & Shapiro (HBR 2010), “Competing on Talent Analytics” — the article that put People Analytics on the executive map.
  • Rasmussen & Ulrich (2015), “Learning from practice: how HR analytics avoids being a management fad” — the warning that analytics teams must connect to actual business decisions.
  • Marler & Boudreau (2017) — systematic review attempting to define what HR analytics actually is.
  • Tursunbayeva, Di Lauro & Pagliari (2018) — broader systematic review of the field.
  • Rynes, Colbert & O’Boyle (2018) — the research–practice gap article.
  • Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, Storytelling with Data — the practical reference for everything in §08 and §09.

Glossary

Terms you should be able to deploy without hesitation.

ACAI
Ask, Collect, Analyse, Influence. The iterative spiral that drives analytics-function maturity.
Algorithmic aversion
Distrust of algorithmic recommendations specifically (as opposed to general resistance to evidence). Often persists even when the algorithm is empirically more accurate than human judgement.
Bing, Bang, Bongo
The three-step communication structure: preview, deliver, summarise. Used to consolidate audience memory under conditions of competing attention.
Confounder
A third variable that affects both predictor and outcome, producing a spurious association.
Construct vs measure
The construct is what you care about (engagement, leakage). The measure is the variable you actually have. They are not the same.
Data minimisation
GDPR principle: only collect data necessary for the stated purpose.
Disparate impact
A practice neutral on its face that produces unequal outcomes across protected groups.
Ecological fallacy
Inferring individual-level relationships from group-level data.
Effect size vs statistical significance
Effect size is magnitude; significance is whether the result is unlikely under a null hypothesis. A significant tiny effect can be practically irrelevant.
Evidence-based management
The movement to ground managerial decisions in best available evidence rather than fashion or intuition.
Four Waves
Benchmarking → Best Practices → Predictive → Guidance for Impact. The strategic-purpose evolution of the analytics function.
Goodhart’s law
When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a useful measure.
Guidance for Impact
The Fourth Wave. Prescriptive, stakeholder-centric analytics that answers the “so that” question.
Horizontal logic
Slide titles tell the story end-to-end. The deck should be readable from titles alone.
Jiu-jitsu persuasion
Aligning your message with the underlying motivation behind a stakeholder’s resistance, rather than attacking the surface attitude.
Mediator
A variable on the causal path between predictor and outcome. The mechanism.
Moderator
A variable that changes the strength or direction of the relationship between predictor and outcome.
Multilevel model
A regression that accounts for the nested structure of data (employees within teams within units).
Operationalisation
The choice of how to translate a construct into a measurable variable. Always partial, always contestable.
Preattentive attributes
Visual features (size, colour, position, line width) that the brain processes before conscious attention. The currency of good data visualisation.
Push vs pull
Push: capability of the analytics team. Pull: organisational demand for evidence-based insight. Both must grow together for an analytics function to mature.
Purpose limitation
GDPR principle: data collected for one purpose cannot be repurposed without justification.
Research–practice gap
The persistent failure of robust academic findings to translate into managerial practice. Rynes et al. (2018) is the canonical reference.
Say–do gap
Self-reported attitudes do not match observed behaviour. The reason analytics should triangulate survey data with behavioural data.
Stakeholders of value
The seven groups (investors, customers, communities, employees, board, executives, HR) whose value defines the outcomes analytics should serve.
Three-Act Structure
Setup, development, resolution. The narrative spine of an effective analytical presentation.
Two-sided argument
Presenting opposing arguments and refuting them with evidence. More persuasive than one-sided advocacy for contested findings.
Vertical logic
Within a slide, everything supports the title. Text supports title, visualisation supports text. No extraneous content.
Z-pattern
Audiences scan pages in a zigzag “Z” starting top-left. Put the most important information in prime real estate.
